\begin{abstract}
The Synthetic Vision System (SVS) is a distributed aircraft safety system originally developed by NASA as a navigational aid for vision-impaired environments. Various SVS are offered on proprietary access or through subscription-based models. Costs can range up to thousands of dollars, which for private pilots may be an unrealistic buy-in. In this work we present the development of an open-source SVS on embedded systems hardware, specifically the Raspberry Pi 5. Primarily written in Go and utilizing the Vulkan Graphics API for rendering, our SVS retrieves and stores public-use data, which is then rendered on a chunk-based system within the simulation and displayed in real-time to the pilot. Initial benchmarking of the system indicates a stable 60 frames-per-second on the Raspberry Pi 5, worldwide terrain and road generation, selectable UI features, and integration of U.S. obstructions and airspaces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\lettrine{M}{odern} aircraft cockpits are frequently equipped with a Synthetic Vision System (SVS), technology that displays a simulated environment of the pilot's surroundings comparable to how it would manifest in clearly-visible conditions \cite{nasa2009svs}. Stored terrain and environment data is combined with real-time GPS and sensor readings to map the current location and positioning of the aircraft and create an accurate picture on the simulated display. On normal flights, SVS improves pilot performance by increasing general situational awareness and reducing mental workload. The SVS also prevents potential controlled-flight-into-terrain (CFID) accidents, where the pilot is in full control of the aircraft and collides into terrain typically as a result of poor visibility \cite{nasa2003cfit}. Development of SVS's began in the 1970s from NASA with a Path-in-the-Sky (PITS) display design, leveraging the developing technology of cathode-ray-tubes and microprocessors to conceptualize a system that would provide a pilot with air position and flight information relative to a pre-programmed path \cite{nasa1977pits}. NASA continued developmental research throughout the 2000s, and now a variety of entities supply specific SVS technology. Currently, SVS for individual pilots costs thousands of dollars; leading provider Aspen Avionics ranges in cost from \$9,995 to \$21,195 \cite{aspenavionicspricing}. Subscription-based models are also prevalent, with ForeFlight's SVS for mobile devices priced at \$260 annually \cite{foreflightpricing}. In this paper, we present the development and release of an open-source SVS designed for use by private pilots on resource-constrained embedded hardware, specifically the Raspberry Pi 5. The use of open data and the relatively low cost of the Raspberry Pi 5 aim to alleviate the current financial burden on private pilots seeking an SVS. In the future, our goal is to create a no-cost mobile application that functions as an SVS by leveraging the GPS and sensor data present inside modern smartphones.

The SVS is built in Go (golang) using Vulkan graphics API to render the simulation. These were chosen for the low-level control they allow, alongside memory safety and synchronization. The SVS has two main components: the downloader and the simulation. As the SVS will not be able to rely on internet connection when in use, all data must be downloaded beforehand. This includes terrain data sourced from NASA, road data sourced from OSM, and obstruction and airspace data sourced from the FAA. A user command will specify the region and type of data to download.\\
%Discuss format of terrain data, road data, airspace data
Obstruction data is sourced from the FAA's Digital Obstacle File (DOF), updated every 56 days. Relevant fields, specifically location, type of obstruction, and quantity, are stored for each obstruction, which is grouped into coordinate squares. Each obstruction coordinate square is then marshaled into a protocol buffer (protobuf). When the simulation is run, relevant coordinate squares are unmarshaled and rendered as their chunks are loaded. The shape of each obstruction is rendered according to its type; typically an appropriately-sized pyramid or rectangular prism. If multiple obstructions are present in one location, they are sized down and rendered equidistant the given centralized point.
Airspace data is sourced from the FAA and filtered by user-specified parameters such as airspace class and state. Boundaries are then provided in GeoJSON format, a standardized geographic data structure for boundaries as coordinate-based polygons. This boundary data is grouped into coordinate-based chunks similar to those used for roads and obstructions. When the simulation is run, each chunk is checked for airspaces, which are then rendered semi-transparent as they load. Each airspace is built by drawing its top and bottom polygons and connecting their edges to form a closed cylinder, and colored depending on its class, giving pilots an intuitive visual reference for the type and extent of controlled airspace.

The simulation component is what actually runs during a flight. At a high-level, it converts all the stored data into Vulkan-friendly vertex and index buffers to get rendered on the display. Due to the large amounts of data and the extended amount of time the SVS aims to run for, rendered data is portioned out with a chunking system and level-of-detail (LOD) rendering.
\\The SVS display is updated upon each new GPS reading according to time, position, and velocity. Because GPS readings do not continuously arrive, the simulation must be adjusted so motion is smoothed and avoids delay. The SVS uses kinematics to extrapolate acceleration and angular velocity and creates two forward predictions that form a three-dimensional curve through space. Interpolation is done with a Catmull-Rom spline with four control points: past reading anchor at \(P_0\), newest reading \(P_1\), first forward prediction \(P_2\), and prediction two GPS intervals in the future \(P_3\). A spline is constructed such that the motion along the spline only occurs between \(P_1\) and \(P_2\). The average interval of seconds between readings is updated each GPS fix and motion along the spline is timed so moving between \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) takes the same amount of time. Each new fix creates a new spline with updated values. If the newest GPS fix arrives later than expected, movement is extrapolated according to motion parameters at \(P_2\). When the fix does arrive, a new spline is constructed and the initial forward motion is blended with the extrapolation to fit the new spline. If no valid GPS fixes have been received in 5 seconds, position is assumed to have continued along the last known trajectory. Should the fix come late, movement is extrapolated according to motion parameters at \(P_2\) and when the fix does arrive, a new spline is constructed and initial forward motion after the reading blends the extrapolated position such that it eventually fits the new spline. If no valid GPS fixes have been received in the last 5 seconds, the pilot's position is assumed to continue along the last known trajectory.\\
\\
The User Interface (UI) of the SVS was built with "Dear-Imgui", a lightweight and widely-used graphical user interface library that supports Vulkan. As the library is written in C++ and our SVS is written in Go, integration required the use of a wrapper. Go wrapper support for Vulkan is generally poor, but we selected "cimgui-go" due to its modular design and support of multiple other graphics APIs. We added Vulkan support to the wrapper and built a basic UI framework that allowed for a components-based approach to the creation of UI elements. With this framework, a collection of flight instruments were implemented into the SVS, including a compass, altimeter, speedometer, and attitude indicator.

The current version of the SVS runs at a stable 60 frames-per-second on the Raspberry Pi 5. It is able to generate terrain and roads worldwide, and obstructions and airspaces within the U.S. and several select surrounding areas, such as parts of Canada and Mexico. The user can also select flight instruments, such as a compass, altimeter, speedometer, and attitude indicator, to be displayed in the simulation. Implementation of a terrain safety indicator changes the color of terrain appropriate to how physically close the aircraft is. Additionally, the simulation can be experienced in one of two modes: head on, or with a birds-eye view of the aircraft.

\section{Conclusion} % Could also have 'Discussion' section before this
Overall, our SVS stands as a viable open-source alternative to the current market options available for purchase. On resource-constrained hardware like the Raspberry Pi 5, we have developed an SVS capable of rendering accurate terrain, roads, obstructions, and airspaces according to the plane's current position and orientation, with UI elements accurately reflecting this. The downloader portion retrieves and stores the relevant data according to user input, marshaling large sets into protocol buffers. The simulation portion utilizes the Vulkan Graphics API to accurately render the data in the displayed environment, utilizing a chunking and LOD system to efficiently manage memory and ensure smooth performance. Currently, the SVS consistently runs at a standard 60 frames-per-second on the Raspberry Pi 5. In the future we endeavor to move the SVS beyond the Raspberry Pi 5 to a mobile application for even further accessibility. Additionally, we plan to further develop interpolation procedures for the SVS to fall back on IMU readings and dead reckon in the case of heavily delayed GPS readings. We also plan to source further data for obstructions and airspaces to render them worldwide, as they are currently sourced from the FAA.
